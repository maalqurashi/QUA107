Why learn this? From guessing to predicting
LO 6.1 · Discrete probability distributions

In business we plan for a future we cannot see. A probability distribution turns the guessing into a model: every possible outcome, each with its probability. Three questions from this chapter:

01
How many customers should we expect?
The mean of a distribution is the long-run average: the number to plan around.
02
What is the chance a batch has too many defects?
The binomial distribution answers it from just two numbers: the sample size and the defect rate.
03
Will a random team have the right mix of people?
The hypergeometric distribution counts the possible teams. And the Poisson distribution counts events in time: calls, arrivals, breakdowns.
Probability distributions are a core tool of business analytics: companies such as stc, Almarai and SABIC use them daily to manage risk and plan capacity. This chapter builds the tool from a table you can read.

What is a random variable?
LO 6.1 · Discrete probability distributions
Definition

A random variable is a variable whose value is a numerical outcome of a random experiment. We write it with a capital letter, usually X; the small letter x stands for one particular value it can take.

Chapter 5 gave us experiments and outcomes. A random variable simply attaches a NUMBER to each outcome, so we can compute with it.

Roll a fair dice: the number that shows is a random variable with the values 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6.
Example: cups of coffee

We ask one KSU student how many cups of coffee they drink per day. The answer is a random variable X: its value could be 0, 1, 2, 3 and so on. Before we ask, we do not know which value we will get; that is what makes it random.

The idea to keep
Experiment → outcome → a NUMBER. The random variable is the number, before we know it.
A customer leaves a delivery review of 1 to 5 stars. What is the random variable, and which values can it take?
The number of stars the next review gives: a random variable with the possible values 1, 2, 3, 4, 5.

What is a discrete probability distribution?07 / 39
What is a discrete probability distribution?
LO 6.1 · Discrete probability distributions
Definition

A discrete probability distribution is a table, graph or formula that lists ALL possible values x of a discrete random variable, and the probability P(x) of each value.

It is the complete picture of the experiment: every value that can happen, and how likely each one is. The frequency tables of Chapter 2 counted the past; a probability distribution predicts the future in the same shape.

Where do the probabilities come from?
Any of Chapter 5's three approaches: classical counting, past data, or judgment.
Rule 1 · every probability is between 0 and 1
0≤P(x)≤1
Rule 2 · the probabilities add to exactly 1
∑P(x)=1

The list covers ALL possible values, and one of them must happen.

A colleague proposes this distribution: P(0) = 0.30, P(1) = 0.50, P(2) = 0.30. Is it valid?
No. Each probability is between 0 and 1 (Rule 1 holds), but the sum is 0.30 + 0.50 + 0.30 = 1.10, not 1: Rule 2 fails. It is not a probability distribution.

The mean of a distribution: the expected value09 / 39
The mean is the expected value
LO 6.1 · Discrete probability distributions
Figure 2The mean is the balance point of the distribution: the spikes balance at μ = 1.40

The mean of a probability distribution is the long-run average of the random variable: the average we would see over many, many visits. We write it μ, or E(X), read: the expected value of X. Both names, one number.

The formula
μ=E(X)=∑[x⋅P(x)]

It is a weighted average of the values: each value x is weighted by its probability P(x). Values that happen often count for more.

Compare with Chapter 3: the mean of DATA weighted each value by its frequency. Here the weights are probabilities: the same idea, facing the future.

The variance and the standard deviation11 / 39
The variance and the standard deviation
LO 6.1 · Discrete probability distributions
Figure 3Each value sits at a distance x − μ from the mean; the variance averages the squared distances, weighted by probability

Two distributions can share the same mean and still behave very differently. The variance σ2 and the standard deviation σ measure the spread of the distribution around its mean: how far the outcomes typically land from μ. More spread means more uncertainty, and in business, more risk.

The formulas
σ2=∑[(x−μ)2⋅P(x)]
σ=σ2​

The same recipe as the mean: a weighted average, but of the squared distances (x−μ)2. The standard deviation brings the answer back to the units of X, so it is the easier one to read.

When do we use the binomial?
LO 6.2 · The binomial distribution

Use the binomial distribution when an experiment repeats the same yes-or-no trial a fixed number of times, and asks: how many of the trials end in success? All four conditions must hold:

1
Fixed trials

The number of trials n is decided in advance: 5 boxes, 10 calls, 20 customers.

2
Two outcomes

Each trial ends in one of TWO outcomes: success or failure, yes or no, leaky or sealed.

3
Constant probability

The probability of success p is the SAME for every trial.

4
Independent trials

The result of one trial does not change the chances of another.

A note on words: success is just the outcome we are counting, even when it is bad news: a leaky box counts as a success in a quality test. Some books write the Greek letter π (pi) for the success probability; we write p. Same number, different letter.

Example: Almarai quality control
LO 6.2 · The binomial distribution

A quality manager at an Almarai factory has a standard: take a random sample of 5 juice boxes; if 2 or more have a leak, stop the whole batch and inspect it, which is very expensive. History says the probability that any single box leaks is 10%.

01
The trial
Inspect one box: leaky or sealed. Two outcomes.
02
The numbers
Sample size n = 5 boxes; probability of a leak p = 0.10, the same for every box.
03
The random variable
X = the number of leaky boxes in the sample. Possible values: 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5.
The question
How often will the quality test stop production? The manager needs P(X≥2). First step: P(X=k) for one value, k = 2.
Check the four binomial conditions for this test.
Fixed trials: n = 5, decided in advance. Two outcomes: leaky or sealed. Constant p: 0.10 for every box off a long production line. Independent: one box's leak does not affect another. All four hold: binomial.

LO 6.2 · The binomial distribution
The formula · three parts
P(X=k)=C(n,k)⋅pk⋅(1−p)n−k
01
C(n,k)
The number of WAYS to place k successes among n trials: the combinations of Chapter 5.
02
pk
The probability of the k successes happening.
03
(1−p)n−k
The probability of the remaining n − k trials being failures.
Almarai, k = 2 leaky boxes

With n = 5, p = 0.10 and k = 2:

C(5,2)=2!(5−2)!5!​=10
P(X=2)=10×(0.10)2×(0.90)3=10×0.01×0.729=0.0729

About a 7.3% chance of finding exactly 2 leaky boxes in a sample of 5. One value done; the distribution has four more.

Shortcuts: the mean and variance of a binomial21 / 39
Shortcuts: mean and variance
LO 6.2 · The binomial distribution

A binomial distribution is still a discrete distribution, so the LO 6.1 formulas work: list the six values, multiply, add. But the binomial's regular shape collapses all that work into two shortcuts:

The shortcuts
μ=npσ2=np(1−p)

The mean reads naturally: n trials, each succeeding a fraction p of the time, average np successes. The standard deviation is σ=np(1−p)​.

Excel=5*0.10the mean needs no special function; SQRT(5*0.10*0.90) gives σ
Almarai, n = 5, p = 0.10
μ=5×0.10=0.50σ2=5×0.10×0.90=0.45σ=0.67

On average a sample holds 0.5 leaky boxes, with a typical spread of about 0.67 around that. A sample with 2 leaks sits more than two standard deviations above the mean: rare, exactly as the 7.3% said.

A call script converts p = 0.05 of calls. An agent makes n = 20 calls a day. How many sales per day should the manager expect?
The binomial mean: μ=np=20×0.05=1 sale per day. Expecting more than that from the script is hoping, not planning.

When do we use the hypergeometric?
LO 6.3 · The hypergeometric distribution
Figure 6Sampling without replacement from a small population: the chances change with every pick

Use the hypergeometric distribution when you sample without replacement from a small, finite population, and count the successes in the sample.

01
Binomial
The success probability is the SAME on every trial: p never moves.
02
Hypergeometric
Each pick REMOVES a person or item, so the success probability CHANGES from pick to pick: P(first pick)=2012​, then 1911​ if an Engineer went first.

Condition 3 of the binomial (constant p) fails, so the binomial formula is the wrong tool here.

LO 6.3 · The hypergeometric distribution

A manager at SABIC must form a project team of 5 people from her department of 20: 12 Engineers and 8 Analysts. She wants a mix of skills; a team with 4 or more Engineers would be too technical. If she picks the 5 names at random, what is the risk of an unbalanced team?

01
N = 20
The population: all 20 employees of the department.
02
S = 12
The successes IN THE POPULATION: the 12 Engineers.
03
n = 5
The sample: the 5 people picked for the team.
04
k = 4
The successes IN THE SAMPLE we ask about: exactly 4 Engineers first.
The question
What is P(X≥4), the probability of 4 or 5 Engineers on a random team? First step: exactly 4.
Why is this not binomial?
The team is drawn without replacement from only 20 people: after each pick the pool changes, so the probability of picking an Engineer changes. No constant p, no binomial.
QUA 107 · Chapter 6 · Discrete Probability DistributionsHypergeometric formula
The hypergeometric formula, by hand26 / 39
The hypergeometric formula, by hand
LO 6.3 · The hypergeometric distribution
The formula · counting teams
P(X=k)=C(N,n)C(S,k)×C(N−S,n−k)​

Pure Chapter-5 counting: the top counts the teams we WANT (k successes chosen from the S, times the n − k failures chosen from the N − S); the bottom counts ALL possible samples of n from N.

Read it as
teams with exactly k Engineers, divided by all possible teams.
SABIC, k = 4 Engineers

Choose 4 of the 12 Engineers, 1 of the 8 Analysts, out of all teams of 5 from 20:

P(X=4)=C(20,5)C(12,4)×C(8,1)​=15,504495×8​=15,5043,960​=0.2554

About a 25.5% chance that a random team has exactly 4 Engineers. Already a substantial risk, and k = 5 is still to come.

Excel=COMBIN(12,4)*COMBIN(8,1)/COMBIN(20,5)the formula assembled from Chapter 5 pieces → 0.2554

A quality engineer samples 3 items from a shipment of 10,000 with 2% defectives. Hypergeometric or binomial, and why?
Technically hypergeometric, but 3 of 10,000 is far below the 5% rule of thumb: use the binomial with n = 3, p = 0.02. The removals cannot move p.

When do we use the Poisson?
LO 6.4 · The Poisson distribution

Use the Poisson distribution to count how many times an event happens over a fixed interval of time, area, distance or volume. There is no fixed number of trials: in one minute, 0 calls can arrive, or 3, or 11.

01
Same chances everywhere
Two intervals of the same length have the same chance of an event.
02
Independent intervals
What happens in one minute does not change the next minute.
03
A known average rate
We know the MEAN number of events per interval: the Greek letter λ, read lambda.
Examples:
01
Per time
Calls reaching a service desk per minute; customers entering a Panda store per hour; trucks arriving at a Jeddah port gate per day.
02
Per distance or area
Paint scratches per metre of pipe; potholes per kilometre of road; typos per page of a report.
A bakery sells an average of 2 whole cakes per hour. Which distribution models the number of cakes sold in the next hour, and with which parameter?
Poisson: events counted over a time interval with a known average rate. λ = 2 cakes per hour.

Example: the call centre
LO 6.4 · The Poisson distribution

The manager of a small customer-service call centre knows the desk receives an average of 5 calls per minute. She has 3 employees on shift, and each can handle one call per minute. If MORE than 3 calls arrive in a minute, callers wait and service suffers.

01
The interval
One minute at the service desk.
02
The rate
λ = 5: the average number of calls per minute.
03
The random variable
X = the number of calls in the next minute: 0, 1, 2, ... with no upper limit.
The question
How often is the desk overwhelmed? The manager needs P(X>3). First step: P(X=3), one exact value.
Why is this not a binomial problem?
There is no fixed number of trials: calls simply arrive over time. We know the average rate, not an n and a p. That is exactly the Poisson situation.

The formula
P(X=k)=k!λke−λ​
01
λ
The average number of events per interval: 5 calls.
02
k
The number of events we ask about: 3 calls.
03
e
The mathematical constant e ≈ 2.71828, the base of the natural logarithm. Every calculator has it; e−λ shrinks fast as λ grows.
The call centre, k = 3
P(X=3)=3!53×e−5​=6125×0.006738​=0.1404

About a 14.0% chance of exactly 3 calls in a minute: the desk fully busy but not yet overwhelmed.

One rate builds everything: put k = 0, 1, 2, ... into the same formula and the whole distribution appears. That is all the Poisson needs.

LO 6.4 · The Poisson distribution
Figure 10The desk is overwhelmed in the blue region: more than 3 calls in a minute
Excel=POISSON.DIST(k, λ, cumulative)events, the mean rate, then FALSE for exactly k, TRUE for k or fewer

More than 3 is the complement of 3 or fewer, so one cumulative call finishes it: =POISSON.DIST(3, 5, TRUE) → 0.2650, since P(X≤3)=P(0)+P(1)+P(2)+P(3).

P(X>3)=1−P(X≤3)=1−0.2650=0.7350
Decision insight
The desk is overwhelmed 73.5% of all minutes: three employees are clearly not enough. The model turns a staffing complaint into a number.

Q1
A website gets an average of 4 support tickets per day. What is the probability of exactly 1 ticket tomorrow?

Q2
With λ = 2 complaints per week, what is the probability of a week with NO complaints?
P(X = 0) = P(X=0)=0!20e−2​=e−2=0.1353: about 13.5% of weeks are complaint-free.
Q3
Back at the call centre (λ = 5): how many employees would keep the risk of being overwhelmed below 10%?
Try 7 employees: P(X > 7) = 1−P(X≤7)=1−0.8666=0.1334, still 13.3%. Try 8: P(X > 8) = 1−P(X≤8)=1−0.9319=0.0681, about 6.8%. Eight employees bring the risk under 10%.
Q4
A fabric factory finds an average of 0.2 defects per square metre. A customer orders 25 square metres. What is λ for the whole order, and the chance of zero defects?
Rates scale with the interval: λ = 0.2 × 25 = 5 defects per order. P(X = 0) = e⁻⁵ = 0.0067: almost every order of this size has at least one defect.

Chapter summary · choosing the distribution38 / 39
Choosing the distribution
SUMMARY
Figure 11Choosing the discrete distribution, by the shape of the situation
The situation chooses the distribution. Any list of values with probabilities is a discrete distribution: its mean is μ=∑x⋅P(x) and its spread comes from the squared distances. Counting successes in fixed yes-or-no trials is binomial (n, p). Sampling without replacement from a small population is hypergeometric (N, S, n). Counting events over an interval is Poisson (λ alone). Excel: BINOM.DIST, HYPGEOM.DIST, POISSON.DIST, each with the same FALSE/TRUE switch.
